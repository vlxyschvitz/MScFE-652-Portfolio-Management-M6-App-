Overview

A discipline testing far more hypotheses than it admits

Factor investing began with a small number of characteristics — size, value, momentum — that looked like compensation for bearing risk. It has since become an industry that publishes a new factor roughly every fortnight, packages the survivors as index products, and sells them to investors who arrive after the returns have already been earned. Module 6 works through the four places this breaks down: the statistics of searching a large space, the economics of everyone crowding into the same trade, the promise and the limits of handing the search to a machine, and the surprisingly large effect of construction choices that never appear in the paper's abstract.

Lesson 1

Profitable anomalies, or anomalous profits

A t-statistic of 2 means there is a one-in-twenty chance of seeing this result if the factor were worthless. That is a reasonable standard when you test one hypothesis. It is a catastrophic standard when the profession has tested several hundred and only publishes the winners. The arithmetic below is the whole argument: hold the false-discovery rate fixed, and the required t-statistic climbs with the size of the search.

t ≈ SR · √T   ·   FDR = M(1 − π)·αM(1 − π)·α + Mπ·(1 − β)
A strategy's t-statistic is its Sharpe ratio scaled by the square root of the sample length in years. The false discovery rate is the share of published factors that are noise: false positives over all positives, where π is the fraction of tested factors that are genuinely profitable and 1 − β is the power to detect them.

Mining a backtest

Every strategy here is worthless by construction: pure noise, zero expected return, no skill anywhere. The only thing that happens is that a large number of them are tried and the best one is kept. Watch the winner's backtest — and then watch what it does next.

The expected maximum of N independent standard normals grows roughly like √(2 ln N). That is why the best of five hundred coin-flip strategies reliably looks like a genuine discovery, and why the number of trials has to be reported for a Sharpe ratio to mean anything.

The question to ask of any factor: not "is the t-statistic significant" but "significant against what search". A factor discovered by an economic argument and then tested once is a different object from a factor found by scanning a database, even when the two produce identical numbers.

Lesson 2

Smart beta, herding, and not-so-smart beta

Smart beta took factor research and turned it into a product: transparent rules, index-fund fees, daily liquidity. That packaging created a new problem. When money floods into a factor, it bids up the price of exactly the securities the factor holds. The inflow shows up as performance, the performance attracts more inflow, and the strategy's valuation runs away from its history — right up until it does not.

realised return = structural return + revaluation return
Arnott and co-authors' decomposition. Only the first term is repeatable. The second is a one-off transfer from tomorrow's return to today's, and it reverses if the valuation spread mean-reverts.

A crowding cycle

A simple feedback loop: performance attracts flows, flows push prices, higher prices raise today's return and lower tomorrow's. Nothing about the underlying factor changes at any point — the boom and the bust are entirely a product of the money chasing it.

Valuation is measured as the spread between what the factor's long leg and short leg trade at, relative to its own history. Zero is normal; positive means the strategy has become expensive relative to how it has historically been priced.

Two failure modes, one label: not-so-smart beta is a product that does not deliver the exposure on the tin, because a naive screen loads on whatever happens to correlate with the signal. Crowded beta is a product that does deliver the exposure, at a price that has already discounted the next decade of it. Neutralisation fixes the first. Only valuation discipline touches the second.

Lesson 3

Factor models with machine learning

The classical factor model is a linear regression of returns on a handful of characteristics. Machine learning relaxes two things at once: it lets you use hundreds of predictors without the estimates exploding, and it lets the relationship be nonlinear. Both help. Neither produces the out-of-sample R² you might expect — in the published work the monthly figure is a fraction of one percent, and the interesting question is why that is still worth a great deal of money.

β̂ridge = (X′X + λT·I)−1X′y   ·   E[R²out] ≈ R²true − (1 − R²true)·K/T
Ridge shrinks coefficients toward zero in exchange for a large reduction in their variance. The second expression is the reason it is needed: for ordinary least squares, out-of-sample fit degrades in proportion to predictors per observation, so a model with 100 predictors and 200 months is expected to have negative out-of-sample R² even when the signal is real.

The cost of an extra predictor

Simulated returns generated from a genuinely predictive linear model, fitted with ridge across a range of penalties. Training fit is monotone and misleading; test fit has an interior optimum, and where that optimum sits depends entirely on how many predictors you are asking the data to support.

The test set is a fresh draw from the same data-generating process, so the gap between the two curves is pure overfitting rather than any change in the world.

When the relationship is not a straight line

Here the true expected return depends on two characteristics and on their interaction — a stock has to be cheap and improving before the signal fires. A plain linear model cannot represent that at any sample size. A linear model with the right interaction term written in by hand can, and will beat everything, because it is the true model. The tree has to find the shape without being told it, which costs something but does not require knowing the answer in advance.

Push the interaction to zero and the linear model wins, because the tree is paying for flexibility it does not need. That trade-off is the whole of model selection.

From a tiny R² to a tradeable Sharpe ratio

A monthly cross-sectional R² of half a percent sounds like nothing. Run it through the fundamental law of active management and it is not nothing at all — but only if breadth is large, the signal is implemented faithfully, and costs are contained.

IR ≈ TC · IC · √B   with   IC ≈ √R²
Information ratio equals the transfer coefficient — how much of the signal survives your constraints — times the information coefficient, times the square root of the number of independent bets per year.

Net information ratio against breadth

Gross information ratio grows with the square root of breadth; the cost drag does not shrink, so the net line only lifts off once there are enough independent bets to pay for the trading.
Gross Net of costs Your breadth

What machine learning does and does not change: it makes a large predictor set usable and it lets the response bend, both of which are real gains. It does not create signal, it does not lower the multiple-testing bar — a flexible model searches an enormous hypothesis space, so Lesson 1 applies with more force, not less — and it does not pay your transaction costs.

Lesson 4

Advanced factor construction

Between "value works" and a portfolio you can trade sit a dozen decisions: which universe, which accounting definition, how far to lag the data, how many stocks in each leg, how often to rebalance, how to weight, whether to neutralise, how hard to trim outliers. None of them is the hypothesis. All of them move the answer. Run the same signal through every combination and the reported Sharpe ratio spreads across a range wide enough to contain both a career-making paper and a rejection letter.

Combining signals: integrate, or blend portfolios?

You have three signals. You can average them into one score and build a single portfolio, or build three portfolios and hold a third of each. The usual claim is that integrating is better, because a blended book happily holds a stock that one signal loves and another hates. Here both are built on the same simulated data and scored on the same basis, so you can check whether that is where the gain actually comes from.

ICcombined = w′ic / √(w′Ωw)   maximised at   w ∝ Ω−1ic
Ω is the correlation matrix of the signals. When signals are uncorrelated, equal weighting is close to optimal; as they converge, the optimal weights start taking large offsetting positions and the combination stops adding much.

Return per unit of risk by method

Averaged over 200 independent cross-sections of 600 stocks. Each book is scored on realised long-short return divided by the size of its positions, so a blend is not penalised simply for having offsetting positions cancel out.

Combined IC as the signals converge

With perfectly correlated signals there is only one signal, and every combination method collapses to the same thing.

On this data the two approaches come out close to level, and the blend sometimes edges ahead. That is worth sitting with: when returns are a clean linear function of the signals, a stock that is wonderful on one and terrible on another really does have an average expected return, so there is nothing for integration to fix. The advantage integration has in practice comes from things this model deliberately leaves out — it never buys a stock in one sleeve while selling it in another, so it pays less in trading; and real factor payoffs interact rather than add, so being extreme on the composite means more than being extreme on any single input. The combination gain that is visible here is a different one: weighting three imperfectly correlated signals beats picking the best one, and that gain is large and reliable.

How to read a factor paper after this module: ask how many specifications were run before this one was reported, ask whether the valuation spread was already stretched over the sample, ask what the strategy's exposure looks like on characteristics nobody mentioned, and ask what the result does when the rebalance frequency or the universe changes. A factor that survives all four is worth owning. Most do not get past the first.
